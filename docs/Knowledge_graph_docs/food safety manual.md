## What Is Food Safety

Food safety is a critical aspect of the food industry, encompassing the scientific principles, handling techniques, preparation methods, and storage practices essential for preventing contamination and foodborne illnesses. It focuses on safeguarding food from harmful bacteria, viruses, parasites, and chemical hazards at every stage of its journey - from production to consumption - ensuring that what we eat is safe and healthy. In a restaurant setting, each team member plays a vital role in upholding these safety standards, highlighting the importance of a robust food safety culture. This culture embodies a shared commitment to values, behaviors, and practices that food business operators and their employees adhere to in order to prepare and serve food safely.

### 4 C's to Food Safety

Understanding the 4 C's of Food Safety are essential to practice to ensure proper hygiene from preparation to storage, effectively protecting against foodborne illness and significantly reducing the risk of harmful bacteria.

- **Cleaning:** to effectively prevent the spread of bacteria, it is essential to wash hands, surfaces, and utensils often with soap and water, scrubbing your hands for at least 20 seconds and ensuring that you clean and sanitize as you go to maintain a safe food preparation environment.

- **Cross-Contamination Prevention:** keep raw meat, poultry, seafood, and eggs separate from ready-to-eat foods by utilizing color-coded cutting boards and plates designated for raw produce and meats.

- **Cooking:** to effectively kill harmful bacteria, always cook foods to the proper internal temperature, and make use of a food thermometer to accurately check their doneness.

- **Chilling:** keep perishable food below 40 degrees Fahrenheit (4 degrees Celsius), refrigerate leftovers promptly within 1-2 hours, and adopt effective practices to keep food cold, cool quickly, and handle it safely.

The food industry widely recognizes these four principles as essential foundations for good hygiene practices, ensuring food safety and maintaining public health.

## Governing Laws And Regulations

Food safety in Canada operates within a comprehensive multi-level governance structure, incorporating federal, provincial, and municipal authorities. This framework is designed to assure that food products are safe, accurately labeled, and suitable for human consumption. At the federal level, the Canadian Food Inspection Agency (CFIA) and Health Canada are the primary bodies responsible for establishing and enforcing standards and regulations that govern food safety across the country. These federal bodies play a crucial role in setting baseline requirements for food safety, which are crucial for both protecting public health and maintaining consumer confidence in the food system.

At the provincial level, each province is tasked with implementing and enforcing food safety regulations within its jurisdiction. This includes oversight of restaurants, food service establishments, and retail food stores. The Food Premises Act serves as a foundational regulatory framework, outlining specific provisions to prevent health hazards. Additionally, the Food Premises Regulations mandate that food establishments employ certified food handlers, maintain rigorous sanitation standards, control food temperatures, and implement measures to protect food from contamination. These regulations are critical in ensuring that food businesses operate safely and responsibly.

Local municipalities and public health units enforce these provincial standards through the work of public health inspectors. They conduct regular inspections and ensure compliance with food safety regulations, thereby protecting the public from foodborne illnesses. Alarmingly, statistics indicate that approximately 1 in 8 Canadians becomes ill each year due to foodborne pathogens. This underscores the importance of diligent food safety practices at all levels of governance. In food service operations, the responsibility for food safety does not rest solely on owners or management; rather, it is shared among all employees. Everyone in the food service industry must adhere to safety protocols and undergo appropriate training to ensure compliance and safeguard public health. Ultimately, the legal liability for food safety lies with the owner, operator, and person in charge, who must foster a culture of safety and professionalism within their establishments.

## Personal Hygiene

Maintaining personal hygiene is essential to food safety, as it significantly reduces the risk of transferring pathogens from food handlers to food, thereby minimizing the chances of foodborne illnesses, contamination, and infections.

Here are some important tips to follow to maintain your personal hygiene:

- Shower daily
- Wear clean clothes
- Avoid wearing jewellery
- Store all personal items away from food
- Keep hair tidy and secured
- Know and follow the hygiene rules of the restaurant

Maintaining food safety begins with clean attire, so it's essential to shower daily, keep your fingernails clean - avoiding fake nails or nail polish - and wear comfortable, closed-toe shoes to ensure a hygienic environment.

### Handwashing

To ensure hygienic practices and prevent the spread of germs, it's essential to keep your hands clean by following these effective handwashing steps:

- Wet hands with clean running water.
- Apply soap and rub hands together to create a lather.
- Scrub hands thoroughly for at least 20 seconds, making sure to scrub backs of hands, between fingers, and under fingernails.
- Rinse hands thoroughly under running water.
- Dry hands using a clean paper towel or air dryer.
- Turn off taps using a paper towel to prevent re-contamination.

With proper use, gloves can help reduce spread of bacteria during food preparation. Some key reasons to wear gloves are:

- To prevent the transfer of food allergens
- To handle ready to eat foods
- Improve hygiene perception with guests
- To protect workers from chemicals, food irritations, and temperature irritations

## Foodborne Illness

Foodborne illness, often referred to as food poisoning, occurs when individuals fall ill after consuming food contaminated with harmful micro-organisms, including bacteria, parasites, or viruses. Food poisoning can affect anyone, but it is particularly dangerous for vulnerable groups such as young children, pregnant individuals, the elderly, and those with weakened immune systems, who face a higher risk of experiencing severe illness.

The most common symptoms associated with foodborne illness are:

- Stomach Cramps
- Nausea
- Vomiting
- Diarrhea
- Fever

Other frequently occurring symptoms can include:

- Headache
- Blurred Vision
- Severe Exhaustion
- Paralysis

## Contamination

Contamination is the unwanted presence or introduction of microbial, physical, chemical, or allergenic hazards in food. These substances may have adverse effects on human health, safety, and the environment.

Cross-contamination is a critical food safety concern that can occur in two key ways: direct cross-contamination happens when there is physical contact between a hazardous source and food, while indirect cross-contamination occurs through a vehicle that transfers contaminants from the source to the food.

## Hazards In Food

A food hazard refers to any biological, chemical, or physical agent present in food that can potentially harm consumers. These hazards occur from contamination during production, processing, or handling, such as pathogen growth, chemical residues, or foreign objects.

### Biological Hazards

Biological hazards include microorganisms or toxins such as bacteria, viruses, parasites, fungi and mold. Biological hazards account for most foodborne illnesses reported in Canada.

#### Bacteria

Foodborne illnesses can manifest rapidly after consumption, with acute and immediate effects typically arising within 30 minutes to 72 hours, depending on the specific microorganism involved. Symptoms may include a distressing array of experiences such as nausea, vomiting, abdominal cramps, chills, and even muscle paralysis or breathing difficulties in severe cases. The condition, often referred to as 'foodborne intoxication,' results from ingesting toxins produced by bacteria present on contaminated food products, and recovery can vary widely - ranging from a few days to a week - based on the type of microbe. It's crucial to note that certain bacterial toxins are resilient to cooking methods, which means that prevention through proper food handling and sanitation is vital.

#### Viruses

Virus hazards in food present a considerable risk for foodborne illness, largely stemming from contamination by infected food handlers or polluted water sources. Among the culprits, Norovirus and Hepatitis A are the most prevalent, notorious for their ability to cause widespread outbreaks. While these viruses do not multiply in food, they retain their infectious properties, leading to distressing symptoms such as nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea in those affected. To mitigate these risks, it is essential to adopt key preventive measures, including rigorous hand hygiene practices, cooking food to appropriate temperatures, and ensuring the use of safe water sources. The primary source of contamination often involves the fecal-oral route, underscoring the critical importance of maintaining personal hygiene to thwart the spread of these harmful viruses in our food supply.

#### Parasites

Foodborne parasites, including various worms and protozoa, are a significant concern as they can lead to infections when individuals consume raw or undercooked food and contaminated water. These parasites not only pose immediate health risks - such as severe digestive issues and fever - but can also result in long-term health complications. Common sources of these infections include beef, pork, milk, fish, fruits, and raw vegetables, as well as untreated water sources frequented by animals. While some infections may remain asymptomatic, others can manifest in short-lived acute illnesses or persist in the body, potentially causing chronic health effects that may linger for weeks. To mitigate these risks, it is crucial to adopt preventive measures: cook food to the recommended internal temperatures, freeze raw items at -20 degrees Celsius for at least seven days, and ensure proper hygiene and sanitation practices. Additionally, purchasing food from approved suppliers, thoroughly washing raw fruits and vegetables, and avoiding unpasteurized products can help significantly reduce the likelihood of infection. By staying vigilant and informed, we can protect ourselves from the potential dangers posed by foodborne parasites.

#### Fungi And Mold

Fungi are ubiquitous microorganisms that primarily lead to food spoilage rather than illness, thriving on plant or animal matter, and producing spores that can easily spread through air, water, or insects. While mycotoxins - harmful toxins generated by certain molds under specific environmental conditions - pose potential health risks. Many molds flourish in environments with high moisture, humidity, and warmth, even managing to thrive at refrigerator temperatures and tolerating higher salt and sugar concentrations than bacteria. Although some molds are beneficial, such as those used in the production of certain cheeses, others can produce dangerous mycotoxins that are undetectable to the naked eye and devoid of any specific taste or smell, complicating efforts to identify contaminated food. In addition to possible allergic reactions and respiratory issues, mycotoxins can lead to serious health consequences ranging from acute illness to chronic toxicity affecting various bodily systems. Effective prevention strategies are key, including reducing moisture and oxygen levels, controlling storage conditions, and ensuring food is covered to minimize exposure to mold spores. Interestingly, while hard and firm foods like parmesan cheese, carrots, and bell peppers are less prone to deep mold penetration, careful handling is essential: when removing moldy

sections, it's important to cut at least one inch around and below the affected area while avoiding cross-contamination. Engaging in these practices can significantly mitigate the risks associated with mold in our food supply.

#### Controlling Biological Hazards

The availability of water in food plays a crucial role in supporting the growth of microorganisms, including bacteria, yeast, and molds, which can lead to food spoilage and foodborne illnesses. To effectively control this microbial growth, reducing available water is essential, and this can be achieved through several methods. Drying, for instance, involves physically removing moisture, as seen in dehydrated garnishes, while the addition of solutes like salt or sugar serves as a preservation technique in pickling and making jams. Freezing also plays a role by transforming water into ice, thus inhibiting microbial activity. Additionally, combining approaches - such as salting and drying fish - can further enhance preservation.

Foods high in protein and carbohydrates, including meat, dairy, cooked beans, and various vegetables, are particularly susceptible to microbial growth and should be handled with care. Inspect food for signs of spoilage, including bulging cans, damaged packaging, mold, or abnormal odors, and err on the side of caution by discarding any items that raise safety concerns. When in doubt about food safety, discard questionable items to prevent foodborne illnesses.

### Chemical Hazards

Chemical hazards in food safety encompass a wide range of risks that can seriously impact health, often without any visible or detectable signs. These hazards arise from cleaning, sanitizing, and disinfecting supplies, as well as the presence of toxic metals from utensils and cookware, and pesticides. Safe food handling practices are essential in preventing these dangers, which can include naturally occurring toxins from plants, animals, and microorganisms, as well as chemicals intentionally or unintentionally added to food.

Intentionally added chemicals, such as Yellow #5 or monosodium glutamate, often exceed the acceptable limits set by food safety regulations, while unintentional additives can stem from contamination during food preparation - often due to cleaning agents, pesticides, or environmental pollutants.

The symptoms of chemical poisoning can manifest quickly, ranging from nausea and stomach pain to dizziness and confusion, underscoring the critical importance of adhering to food safety protocols to safeguard public health.

To maintain a safe environment, thorough training on safe handling and application procedures for all chemicals is essential, alongside the diligent practice of washing hands after use. Utilizing food-safe utensils, such as stainless steel, further reduces contamination risks. Acidic foods can dissolve lead-containing or galvanized dishes, leaching harmful metals into the food, causing metal poisoning.

It is crucial to source materials from reputable suppliers who prioritize effective chemical hazard control and to always store chemicals in clearly labeled containers, separate from food items. Following manufacturers' guidelines on chemical use, storage, and cleaning protocols are necessary protocols along with avoiding excessive use of lubricants on equipment and ensuring that all food contact surfaces are thoroughly cleaned post-maintenance.

Effective chemical safety management also requires maintaining easy access to safety data sheets, adhering to good storage practices, and ensuring that flammable materials are kept away from heat sources to minimize risks and promote a safe working environment.

### Physical Hazards

Physical hazards in food are a critical concern for anyone involved in the food industry. The presence of physical contaminants can lead to serious illness or injury, emphasizing the importance of comprehensive safety and control measures throughout the entire food chain - from the field to the plate. These hazards can be introduced at any stage, whether from the origin of the product, raw materials, packaging, or during processing.

Common examples of physical hazards include bone or shell fragments from animal products, dirt and stones found in fruits and vegetables, and metal shards that can occur during processing activities involving cutting or grinding. Additionally, items such as jewelry and personal items may inadvertently make their way into food due to lapses in food handling practices. Other contaminants may stem from the processing environment, like glass from broken containers or wood splinters from packaging, or even insect fragments.

To effectively control these physical hazards, implementing best practices is essential. Washing all fruits and vegetables thoroughly before use is a fundamental step to remove soil and potential contaminants. Utilizing suitable hair restraints, such as hats or hairnets, while preparing food helps prevent hair from falling into dishes, while covering food items protects them from external contaminants.

Maintain cleanliness in food preparation areas by keeping surfaces free from objects that might accidentally fall into food minimizes risks. In cases where glass breakage occurs, it is vital to meticulously clean up all fragments to avoid any potential harm.

When looking at physical hazards, we categorize them by extraneous materials; unavoidable and avoidable hazards. Unavoidable extraneous material may naturally occur as a by-product of the food processing system, such as stems in blueberries or dirt on potatoes, which are generally accepted as part of the product. Conversely, avoidable extraneous material should never be present if proper Good Manufacturing Practices (GMPs) are followed. This includes items like small glass fragments or plastic pieces, which indicate lapses in safety protocols. By understanding the sources and types of physical hazards, we can more effectively protect the health of our guests and ensure a safe dining experience.

## Food Allergies

Food allergies are a serious concern, arising when the immune system mistakenly targets certain food proteins as threats, leading to potentially severe reactions. The immune system normally protects a person from germs and disease. It helps them to fight off bacteria, viruses, and other tiny organisms that can make them sick. If someone has a food allergy, their immune system mistakenly treats something in a particular food (most often, the protein) as if it's dangerous to them. Their body reacts to the food by having an allergic reaction. An estimated 8% of children and 4% of adults have food allergies. There is no cure for allergies, however, some children do outgrow them.

### Symptoms

For some individuals, an allergic reaction to specific foods can manifest as a mild discomfort, leading to symptoms such as tingling or itching in the mouth, hives, or occasional gastrointestinal distress like belly pain or nausea. While these reactions may be bothersome and inconvenient, they often do not escalate into life-threatening situations. However, the experience of food allergies can vary dramatically from person to person. For others, even a small exposure to an allergenic food can trigger a severe and frightening reaction.

Symptoms typically emerge within minutes to a couple of hours after consumption, but there are rare instances where they may be delayed. The most alarming aspect of food allergies is the potential for anaphylaxis, a rapid and severe allergic reaction that can be life-threatening. During anaphylaxis, individuals may experience constricted airways, which makes breathing difficult, and a sense of impending doom as their body goes into shock, causing a dramatic drop in blood pressure and a rapid heartbeat. Symptoms can escalate quickly, resulting in dizziness or loss of consciousness. Because anaphylaxis can progress rapidly and become fatal if left untreated, immediate medical attention is essential.

Many people who have a history of anaphylaxis reactions carry an epinephrine auto-injector. Being educated on the signs of anaphylaxis can mean the difference between life and death for those at risk. Often the friends and family of someone with a risk of anaphylaxis will know where the person's auto-injector is and are informed on how to use it.

### Common Food Allergies

You can be allergic to any food, but some allergies are more common than others. Health Canada partnered with the medical community, allergy associations, and the food industry to strengthen labelling requirements for priority allergens, gluten sources, and sulphites in foods sold across the nation. These regulations ensure that the most common allergens are identified by their common names, so they are easily identified. Health Canada lists 10 foods as "priority food allergens", as these foods are associated with 90% of allergic reactions in Canada.

#### Crustaceans And Mollusks

Crustaceans and mollusks are often collectively referred to as shellfish. Crustaceans, including crab, lobster, and shrimp, are characterized by their jointed legs and hard shells, while mollusks, such as clams and scallops, typically feature a hinged two-part shell. Interestingly, allergies to these are relatively rare among young children but adults may develop sensitivities later in life and can lead to them having serious reactions. What makes crustacean and mollusk allergies particularly concerning is that reactions can occur even without direct consumption; exposure to cooking vapors, such as those from boiling lobster, or residue on utensils and serving dishes can trigger allergic responses in sensitive individuals.

#### Fish

An important note is that while fish, crustaceans, and mollusks are often referred to collectively as seafood, people who are allergic to one type. Many people are only allergic to a single type of seafood. Some people can eat lobster safely but have allergic reactions to fish. In North America, fish allergies are predominantly observed in adults. In contrast, in regions where fish is a dietary staple, these allergies are prevalent among both adults and children, highlighting the impacts dietary habits can have on allergic reactions. Once developed, fish allergies are often lifelong, with reactions typically occurring within minutes of exposure, although they can sometimes manifest several hours later. The most severe response, anaphylaxis, can pose life-threatening risks. Along with crustaceans and mollusks, individuals with fish allergies can trigger reactions even without direct consumption. Some common culprits you may see include anchovies, salmon, tuna, haddock, halibut, and cod, among others.

#### Egg

Eggs possess two allergenic components - the yolk and the white - that can trigger allergic reactions in sensitive individuals. While many children may outgrow their sensitivity to eggs within a few years, for some, this allergy can persist into adulthood.The proteins found in chicken eggs are similar to those in the eggs of ducks, geese, quails, and other birds, which means that individuals allergic to chicken eggs may also react to eggs from these other fowl. However, some individuals with an egg allergy can tolerate extensively heated or baked goods that contain egg.

#### Milk

Milk allergy is primarily triggered by an abnormal immune response to the proteins found in cow's milk. The proteins found in cow's milk are like those present in the milk of other mammals, including goats, sheep, and even deer and buffalo causing individuals to have reactions to these types of milk as well. For those with a cow's milk allergy, exposure can trigger a variety of immune responses, often leading to uncomfortable - or even life-threatening - reactions. Some people may tolerate extensively heated or baked milk products, as the cooking process alters the proteins enough to reduce their allergenic potential if they are cooked all the way through. While many children may outgrow this allergy within a few years, for others, it can persist into adulthood. It's important to clarify that a milk allergy is distinct from lactose intolerance. Lactose intolerance involves difficulty digesting the milk sugar lactose whereas a milk allergy involves a potentially severe immune reaction to milk proteins, which can be life-threatening.

#### Mustard

Mustard is a member of the Brassicaceae family, which includes vegetables like broccoli, cauliflower, and Brussels sprouts. Those with a mustard allergy must exercise caution not only with mustard seeds but also with the seeds and sprouted seeds of closely related plants, as their proteins can closely resemble those found in mustard. Canola oil can be derived from canola seeds or canola-quality mustard seeds. While highly refined canola is generally safe due to the minimal protein content, individuals should steer clear of cold-pressed canola oil, which retains more protein and could provoke an allergic response. As mustard is a staple ingredient in various cuisines worldwide, ranging from Indian and Middle Eastern to Eastern European and Italian, those affected by this allergy need to be vigilant. It is important to take the time to go through any questions or concerns they may have with them.

#### Peanut

Peanut allergy is one of the most prevalent food allergies, affecting individuals across various age groups and demographics. As members of the legume family, peanuts are distinct from tree nuts, meaning that a person may be allergic to peanuts while being able to consume tree nuts, or vice versa. Recently, the rising use of lupin - a legume similar to peanuts - in gluten-free products has raised concerns, as some individuals may react to both ingredients. Peanut allergy is particularly concerning because it is the leading cause of food-induced anaphylaxis, a severe and potentially life-threatening allergic reaction. Peanut allergies can be triggered by direct consumption of peanuts or foods containing peanuts, unintentional cross-contact during food processing, and even inhalation of peanut dust or aerosols. While it was once believed that peanut allergies were lifelong, emerging research indicates that some children may outgrow their sensitivities over time. Given the integral role of peanuts in many international cuisines, awareness and management of this allergy are vital for those affected, ensuring safe dining experiences.

#### Tree Nuts

Tree nuts include a variety of nuts such as almonds, Brazil nuts, cashews, hazelnuts, macadamia nuts, pecans, pine nuts, pistachios, and walnuts. It's important to note that coconut and nutmeg, despite their names, are not classified as tree nuts; coconut is actually a seed of a fruit, and nutmeg comes from the seeds of a tropical tree. However, some individuals may also have allergies to coconut and nutmeg. Allergic reactions can be swift, often occurring within minutes of exposure to the allergen, though they can sometimes manifest several hours later. The most severe form of an allergic response is anaphylaxis highlighting the importance of the management of tree nut allergies.

#### Sesame

Sesame, a flowering plant known for its edible seeds, has been recognized as a common allergen that poses significant health risks for some individuals. These seeds come in various shades, from creamy white to deep charcoal black, and are celebrated for their rich flavor and nutritional benefits. Sesame oil, known for its dark color and distinctive taste, is a staple in many Asian cuisines. It's important to note that allergic reactions can occur swiftly, often within minutes of exposure to sesame, but in some cases, they may arise several hours later with the most

severe response being anaphylaxis, Given the widespread use of sesame in baked goods such as hamburger buns, breads, and cookies, as well as in dressings, sauces, and even processed meats, awareness of this allergen is vital to avoid potentially dangerous situations.

#### Soy

Soy allergy is a prevalent concern, particularly among infants, as it arises from soybeans, a versatile legume that can be transformed into various products such as soy milk, tofu, flour, and oil. Interestingly, individuals with a soy allergy may not necessarily experience reactions to other legumes, such as peanuts, highlighting the unique nature of this allergy. While many children outgrow soy allergies over time, some may face lifelong challenges. It's worth noting that while refined soy oils are often safe for those with soy allergies because the refining process typically eliminates the proteins responsible for triggering allergic reactions, this is not always the case. Additionally, soy lecithin, a common food additive derived from soy oil, usually contains insufficient protein to cause harm, though some sensitive individuals may still react. Allergic reactions can occur swiftly, often within minutes, but can occasionally manifest several hours post-exposure, with anaphylaxis representing the most severe response to this allergen.

#### Wheat And Triticale

Wheat and triticale, a hybrid of wheat and rye, are recognized as priority food allergens, warranting caution for those with specific grain sensitivities. While triticale may not be widely available in commercial settings, individuals with a wheat allergy should proactively avoid it to prevent adverse reactions. Notably, wheat allergy often manifests in infants and is frequently outgrown, but it can persist in adults. Distinct from celiac disease, which involves an immune-mediated response leading to damage in the small intestine upon gluten ingestion, a wheat allergy triggers an abnormal immune response to wheat proteins, with potentially life-threatening consequences. Additionally, exercise-induced anaphylaxis is notably linked to wheat, where affected individuals may experience severe reactions if they engage in physical activity shortly after consuming wheat products, although delaying exercise can mitigate these risks.

### Food Intolerance

Food intolerance, often referred to as food sensitivity, occurs when your digestive system struggles to break down specific foods, leading to a range of uncomfortable symptoms. Unlike food allergies, which can provoke severe immune responses, food intolerances primarily affect the digestive system, manifesting in issues such as gas, diarrhea, and abdominal pain within a few hours of consumption.

![](assets/internal/barrio/training/food_safety_manual/04.webp)

The most common culprits include:

- **Lactose:** insufficient levels of the lactase enzyme hinder the breakdown of dairy sugars.
- **Histamine:** accumulates in certain foods and can cause reactions in individuals lacking adequate diamine oxidase.
- **Gluten:** a protein that some people find difficult to digest.

Gluten sensitivity is different from celiac disease, which is an autoimmune disease where gluten damages the small intestines.

While the exact causes of food intolerances remain unclear, factors such as certain gastrointestinal conditions - including celiac disease and inflammatory bowel disease - may increase susceptibility. Individuals with food intolerances may notice that consuming small amounts of a troublesome food can sometimes evade symptoms so they may be ok with potential cross-contamination.

## Food Allergies: Keep Your Guests Safe

Every 3 minutes, a food allergy reaction sends someone to the emergency room.

The "top eight" common food allergens are:

- Peanuts
- Tree nuts
- Fish
- Shellfish
- Eggs
- Milk
- Wheat
- Soy

Food allergies are serious. An allergic reaction to food can cause death. When serving a person with a food allergy:

- **Avoid cross-contact from the start.** Clean the dining area with clean soap and water and a clean towel between each guest.
- **Make sure you understand the allergy.** Write down the guest's allergy to record the conversation. If you have questions, ask the guest.
- **Take all food allergy requests seriously.** Refer the allergy to the chef, manager, or person in charge.
- **Use only fresh ingredients, including cooking oil.**
- **Get it there safely.** Double check with the chef to make sure you have the right meal. Do not let anyone add garnishes. Carry the special meal to the table separately.
- **Check with the guest to make sure they are satisfied with their meal.**

Avoid cross-contact. All food equipment that is used in the handling and processing of allergy-safe foods must be properly cleaned and sanitized before use.

Call 911 at the first sign of a reaction.

### What To Do If Someone Has A Reaction

If you witness someone experiencing such a reaction, the first step is to call 911 without delay. When speaking with the operator, clearly inform them that a person is undergoing a severe allergic response. It's vital to assess whether the individual has an EpiPen; if they do, assist them in using it as soon as possible, as timely administration of epinephrine can be life-saving. According to the Canadian Institute of Food Safety, swiftly locating the EpiPen is essential, and if the person is dining with companions, enlist their help in finding it quickly.

While awaiting emergency services, keep the person calm and comfortable. If they are coherent, keep them seated or lying on their back. If they're experiencing vomiting or breathing difficulties, position them on their side, and elevate their feet about 12 inches to facilitate blood flow. It's important to never leave the affected individual alone; ensure a staff member or a companion stays by their side until help arrives. Simultaneously, gather information about what the person has consumed, as this will assist medical professionals. It's important to avoid allowing the person to walk or move around, as this could exacerbate their condition. Refrain from administering oral medications like antihistamines, which are ineffective in severe reactions. Instead, if anaphylaxis is suspected, act with urgency and administer epinephrine without hesitation.

Once the immediate situation is under control, document the incident thoroughly and collect witness statements. Lastly, preserve any food or packaging that may have triggered the reaction for further investigation, ensuring that all aspects of the incident are addressed comprehensively.

## Temperature Danger Zone

In a commercial kitchen, the top priority must always be the understanding and enforcement of food safety temperatures. This critical focus is essential not only for maintaining high culinary standards but also for protecting customers from the risks associated with foodborne illnesses. The importance of temperature control cannot be overstated; foodservice operators and handlers must be well-versed in the concept of the temperature danger zone and the serious implications it presents to public health.

Bacteria thrive in environments where temperatures range from 40 to 140 degrees Fahrenheit (4 to 60 degrees Celsius). This temperature danger zone is particularly hazardous because bacteria can double in number in as little as 20 minutes. As such, the longer food lingers in this temperature range, the higher the risk that harmful bacteria will proliferate, potentially leading to severe health consequences for guests. Perishable items should never be left out of refrigeration for longer than two hours; after that, the risk of bacterial growth increases significantly.

What is particularly alarming about this danger zone is that harmful bacteria can grow to dangerous levels without any visible signs of spoilage. Foods may smell and look perfectly fine, yet they can still harbor harmful levels of bacteria that could lead to foodborne illnesses. In a world where food safety is paramount, understanding and diligently enforcing temperature standards is a non-negotiable part of every successful kitchen operation.

### Time Temperature Abuse

Time temperature abuse is a significant health code violation which occurs when foods are kept in the temperature danger zone, leading to a heightened risk of foodborne illness. This can happen through inadequate storage temperatures, insufficient cooking or reheating to eliminate pathogens, or improper cooling of hot foods before refrigeration.

### Monitor Temperatures

Kitchen thermometers are the key to keeping foods out of the temperature danger zone. It's crucial to continuously monitor your dishes with a probe thermometer, keeping a close eye on recommended temperatures throughout the cooking process, calibrating them at least once a week and after any drops, while also maintaining equipment with built-in temperature sensors or probes to guarantee accurate readings. 

![](assets/internal/barrio/training/food_safety_manual/06.webp)

### Controlling Time and Temperature

To maintain food safety and effectively control time and temperature, it is essential to stir frequently, cook in small batches when feasible, ensure thorough cooking, and minimize open doors to keep food out of the temperature danger zone, while also being mindful that freezing does not kill bacteria and viruses.

## Cooling And Reheating

### Safe Cooling Practices

When it comes to ensuring food safety while cooling large quantities of cooked food, employing safe cooling techniques is essential to prevent bacterial growth and maintain quality. One effective method is to divide large amounts of food into smaller, shallow containers - ideally no deeper than 2 inches - to maximize surface area and expedite cooling. An ice water bath is also a fantastic approach; by immersing pots or containers in a sink filled with ice and water, and stirring frequently, the heat is rapidly drawn out. For substantial items like roasts or turkeys, cutting them into smaller, thinner pieces can significantly speed up the cooling process. Additionally, regularly stirring liquids helps release trapped heat, further quickening the cooling timeline. To enhance ventilation, it's advisable to leave lids slightly cracked while cooling in the fridge, sealing them only once the food has reached room temperature. For those with access to ice paddles, these can be a game-changer for liquids, cooling them from the inside. However, at home, making the most of available tools like cookie sheets to spread out rice or cake pans for soup can facilitate the cooling process effectively. Remember that the goal is to bring foods down from 140 degrees F to 70 degrees F within two hours before safely refrigerating, so monitoring the temperature throughout is crucial to maintaining food safety and quality.

### Two Step Cooling Method

The two-step cooling method is a food safety procedure that rapidly lowers the temperature of cooked food to prevent bacterial growth.

- Freshly cooked foods must be cooled down from 57 degrees C / 135 degrees F to 21 degrees C / 70 degrees F within 2 hours.
- From 21 degrees C / 70 degrees F, the temperature of the food must be further decreased to 5 degrees C / 40 degrees F or less within the next 4 hours.
- The entire cooling process must be done within a maximum time of 6 hours.
- Always monitor the whole cooling process to ensure food safety.
- Keep all cooled foods in the refrigerator or freezer.

### Safe Reheating Practices

When reheating food, it's crucial to ensure that the internal temperature reaches at least 165 degrees F (74 degrees C), a threshold that effectively eliminates most bacteria and neutralizes harmful toxins that may be present. Food safety guidelines dictate that leftovers should be reheated to this temperature for a minimum of 15 seconds, and the total duration that food remains between 41 degrees F and 165 degrees F should not exceed two hours. To promote even heating, especially in a microwave, it's advisable to stir, rotate, or cover the food, and to always use a digital food thermometer for accuracy. Remember to serve reheated food immediately and only prepare what you plan to eat, as re-cooling leftovers can compromise both safety and quality. For soups, gravies, and sauces, bring them to a vigorous boil while stirring to ensure even heating. Additionally, avoid using slow cookers for reheating, and be mindful to discard any food that has been left at room temperature for more than two hours. By adhering to these guidelines, you can enjoy your leftovers safely and deliciously.

### Defrosting Food

Proper defrosting is crucial in reducing the risk of food poisoning, a serious concern that arises when harmful bacteria contaminate our food. To ensure meat, poultry, fish, and seafood are safe to eat, they must be cooked to a safe internal temperature that effectively eliminates these bacteria. However, if these foods are not thawed correctly, bacteria present on the surface before freezing can start to multiply as they warm. For example, cooking meat that is still partially frozen can lead to uneven cooking, meaning some parts may not reach the necessary temperature to kill off harmful pathogens. The safest method for defrosting is to place the food in the refrigerator, ideally on the bottom shelf in a clean container to catch any drippings and prevent cross-contamination with other foods. Generally, it takes about 24 hours for every 2.5 kg (5 pounds) of poultry and typically 24 hours for other meats to defrost completely. Alternatively, food can be defrosted in cold water - either by running cold tap water over it or submerging it in cold water within a leak-proof plastic wrap, ensuring the water is changed every 30 minutes.

## Thermometer Calibration

Controlling temperature in the kitchen is paramount to achieving culinary success, as even slight deviations can lead to significant mistakes in your cooking. However, relying solely on your thermometer can sometimes be misleading, as it may not always provide an accurate reading. To ensure precision, creating a properly made ice bath serves as a straightforward, reliable method to assess your thermometer's accuracy against the freezing point of water (32.0 degrees F/0 degrees C). This simple experiment can help you avoid the pitfalls that arise from an unreliable gauge. Common errors in performing an ice bath calibration include using the wrong ice-to-water ratio, failing to insert the probe deep enough into the mixture, neglecting to stir for even temperature distribution, and using different types of ice that may not freeze uniformly. Addressing these small but impactful mistakes is crucial to maintaining accuracy in your culinary endeavors, empowering you to cook with confidence.

### Four Steps For Creating An Ice Bath

1. Fill your container to the top with ice. Crushed ice is ideal, because it creates a denser environment and a more consistent temperature throughout the bath.
2. Add water to the container until the water reaches about 1/2 inch below the top of the ice. Let the ice slurry sit for a minute to allow the temperature to even out. If the ice rises from the bottom of the container, remove some water and add more ice. The water beneath the ice will have a temperature above 32 degrees F.
3. Place your probe or thermometer tip into the center of the ice slurry and mix gently. Stirring helps spread heat evenly and prevents your thermometer from touching ice, the container walls, or the bottom (which will give you inaccurate temperature readings).
4. In an ice bath, your thermometer should read 32 degrees F (0 degrees C). If it doesn't, follow the manufacturer's adjustment instructions. However, before calibrating a digital, instant-read thermometer, verify that the reading falls within the manufacturer's accuracy range. If it does, adjustment isn't needed.

![Diagram: four steps to a proper ice bath for calibrating a thermometer](assets/internal/barrio/training/food_safety_manual/07.webp)

## Thermometer Ice Bath Calibration

### Critical Steps To Creating A Proper Ice Bath

1. **Fill vessel with ice.** Fill the vessel all the way to the top with ice. Crushed ice is preferred because there are fewer gaps between the ice.
2. **Add correct amount of water.** Fill to about 1/2 inch below the top of the ice. Ice should not be floating off the bottom. If you see the ice starting to float off the bottom of the vessel, pour off some water and add more ice. Water below the ice will not be at 32 degrees F (0 degrees C).
3. **Insert probe, gently stir.** Stir the probe in the vertical center of the ice. Allow sufficient time for the thermometer reading to stabilize. Stirring the probe keeps the sensor from resting against an ice cube, which will affect the reading. Keep the probe tip away from the side walls and do not allow it to rest against the bottom of the vessel. Keep stirring the probe.
4. **Calibrate.** Your thermometer should read 32 degrees F (0 degrees C) in the ice bath. Adjust your dial thermometer as directed by the manufacturer. Before adjusting a digital instant-read thermometer, check that the readings are within the manufacturer's accuracy specifications. If it is within the specified tolerance, do not adjust.

## Safe Storage

### Dry Storage

For the safe storage of dry goods, it is essential to maintain a controlled environment with temperatures between 10-21 degrees Celsius (50-70 degrees F) and humidity levels of 50-55%. To promote optimal freshness, ensure that food items are stored at least 6 inches (15 cm) off the floor and 2 inches (5 cm) away from walls. Avoid exposure to direct sunlight and heat sources, and keep products in their original packaging until opened. Once opened, transfer dry goods to a labelled, airtight container to maintain quality and prevent spoilage. It is also crucial to discard any items that have surpassed their best-before date and to label containers with the delivery date for easy tracking. Following these guidelines will help ensure your dry goods remain safe, fresh, and enjoyable for as long as possible.

### Refrigerator Storage

To ensure safe refrigerator storage and minimize the risk of foodborne illnesses, it's crucial to maintain a temperature at or below 40 degrees F (4 degrees C). To effectively organize your fridge, place raw meat, poultry, and fish in sealed containers on the bottom shelf; this not only prevents harmful drips from contaminating other foods but also adheres to safe food handling practices. Ready-to-eat foods should be stored on the upper shelves, as keeping them higher in the fridge protects them from potential cross-contamination. Proper shelf organization is key: arrange items from lowest cooking temperature to highest as you move down the shelves to reduce the likelihood of pathogen growth.

**Top Shelf: Ready-to-Eat.** The top shelf should be reserved for ready-to-eat foods. These are foods that will be served without being cooked first. 

**Second Shelf: 135 degrees F (57 degrees C).** This category includes foods that will be hot-held that are not included in other categories. 

**Third Shelf: 145 degrees F (63 degrees C).** Foods that should be cooked to 145 degrees F include whole seafood; whole cuts of beef, pork, veal, lamb; roasts; and eggs that will be served immediately.

**Fourth Shelf: 155 degrees F (68 degrees C).** It is important that meat that has been ground, injected, or tenderized be kept on a lower shelf. This category also includes eggs that will be hot held. 

**Bottom Shelf: 165 degrees F (74 degrees C).** The bottom shelf should hold foods with the highest cooking temperatures. This includes all poultry (turkey, duck, chicken, or fowl); stuffing that contains foods that require temperature control; dishes with previously cooked foods, such as casseroles.

While refrigerators are effective in slowing the growth of pathogens, it's essential to remember that they do not halt the spoiling process; thus, knowing when to throw out food, despite the feeling of waste, is crucial for ensuring the safety and health of you and your guests.

**Up to 2 days:** Ground beef, turkey, veal, pork, lamb; stew meats; variety meats; whole chicken or turkey; giblets; raw or poultry sausage; fresh fish and shellfish. 

**Up to 4 days:** Cooked egg dishes; soups and stews; cooked casseroles; gravy, broth, patties, and nuggets; store-cooked dinners and entrees; fully-cooked ham slices. 

**Up to 5 days:** Canned ham (labeled "Keep Refrigerated"); egg, chicken, tuna, ham, and macaroni salads; opened luncheon meats; fully-cooked ham, half. 

**Up to 7 days:** Bacon; smoked sausage links or patties; fully-cooked ham, whole; corned beef in pouch (with pickling juices) 

**Up to 2 weeks:** Unopened hot dog and luncheon meat packages. 

**Up to 3 weeks:** Opened summer sausage packages; hard sausage (such as pepperoni)

## FIFO

FIFO, or First-In, First-Out, is an essential food storage management system that plays a crucial role in maintaining both the quality and safety of food inventory. By ensuring that the oldest items are used or sold before newer stock, FIFO maximizes ingredient freshness and minimizes waste. This system is particularly effective in environments where food safety is paramount, such as restaurants, catering services, and even home kitchens. By organizing shelves and refrigerators so that items with the earliest expiration dates are placed at the front, FIFO not only enhances the culinary experience but also safeguards public health by reducing the risk of foodborne illnesses.

### FIFO Principles

The key principles of the FIFO storage system are straightforward yet vital for effective management. Labeling everything is the first step; accurately marking all ingredients with the date they were received, opened, or prepared helps to establish a clear timeline for usage. Next, rotation is critical: when introducing new items, it's essential to reposition older stock to the front, ensuring they are consumed first. Regular checks for expiration dates and signs of spoilage should be part of routine operations - this proactive approach allows for the timely use of products before they go bad. Additionally, proper storage is fundamental; food should be sealed in appropriate containers, and fridges should not be overcrowded, as this ensures proper airflow and cooling.

### Benefits

Implementing FIFO yields a variety of benefits that can positively impact any food management operation. A significant advantage is the reduction of waste; by adhering to FIFO principles, businesses and households can significantly lower the amount of food that expires before being utilized. Furthermore, enhanced food safety is another critical benefit, as using food in a timely manner reduces the chances of consuming spoiled or unsafe items. Financially, FIFO saves money by minimizing costs associated with discarding expired inventory, ultimately leading to a more efficient operation. An organized storage system fosters smooth inventory management, making it easy to locate and access ingredients when needed.

### Common Mistakes

Implementing FIFO can come with its challenges. One common pitfall is the failure to label items accurately, which can lead to confusion and the inadvertent use of expired products. Another mistake is restocking without proper rotation, where new items are placed in front of older ones, potentially resulting in wasted inventory. Overcrowding of storage spaces is also detrimental; a cluttered environment makes it difficult to see and retrieve older items that are hidden behind new stock. Finally, neglecting to check for spoilage, even when items are within their expiration dates, can compromise food safety.

## Labelling

Labeling is an essential practice in any food preparation environment, serving as a critical way to ensure safety and efficiency within the kitchen. This simple step not only communicates vital information but also contributes to a well-organized workflow, allowing kitchen staff to easily identify ingredients and manage inventory effectively.

One particularly important labeling practice involves open products that are not yet empty. ## Cleaning And Sanitation

Cleaning is defined as the removal of dirt, food, grease, or any debris from a surface, usually with detergent and water. Sanitizing refers to the reduction of most harmful microorganisms and pathogens on a surface to a safe level.

### Five Step Process For Cleaning And Sanitation

1. **Scrape food.** Manually remove all excess food waste and obstacles on the surface.
2. **Wash the surface.** Wash the surface with warm, soapy water at 43 degrees C to remove any oil residue.
3. **Rinse the surface.** Rinse the surface with clean water, making sure that there is no soap residue.
4. **Soak in sanitizer.** Soak the surface in your chosen sanitizer following the manufacturer's instructions. You can wash counters with bleach water or use hot water at 82 degrees C for sanitation.
5. **Air-dry.** Allow the sanitized surface to air dry.

### Important Things To Consider In Sanitation

- If using a sanitizer solution, follow the manufacturer's suggested contact time.
- Never use a towel to dry food contact surfaces.
- Sanitize surfaces before and after every shift.

## HACCP

HACCP, or Hazard Analysis Critical Control Point, is a structured and systematic approach to food safety, ensuring the safety of food products from production to consumption. This methodology is designed to identify potential hazards, whether biological, chemical, or physical, that could compromise food safety. By pinpointing these risks early on, HACCP allows food industry professionals to implement effective hazard control measures, ensuring that food products are safe for guests. The system not only describes what needs to be done to mitigate these risks but also outlines the specific procedures and monitoring activities necessary to maintain safety standards throughout the food handling process.

Food safety relies heavily on principles such as due diligence defense, awareness, compliance, training, and comprehensive food safety management. These elements collectively support the implementation of HACCP by promoting a culture of responsibility and vigilance within food handling environments. Due diligence defense emphasizes the importance of taking all reasonable steps to ensure food safety, while awareness and compliance help foster a proactive mindset among employees. Comprehensive training equips staff with the knowledge they need to recognize hazards and adhere to safety protocols, thus creating a safer food supply chain.

### Key Aspects

One of the key aspects that sets HACCP apart from traditional inspection methods is its preventative focus. Instead of merely reacting to safety issues after they occur, HACCP emphasizes the importance of foresight, aiming to prevent hazards before they emerge. Its versatility makes it applicable across various sectors within the food industry, including manufacturing, processing, and distribution. The benefits of implementing HACCP are manifold: it not only increases consumer confidence by assuring the safety and quality of food products but also reduces the risks associated with product recalls. Additionally, by maintaining consistency in food quality and aligning with regulatory requirements, HACCP helps organizations build a solid foundation for sustainable success in an increasingly competitive market.

### The 7 Principles of HACCP

1. Conduct a Hazard Analysis: Identify potential food safety hazards.
2. Identify Critical Control Points (CCPs): Determine points where hazards can be prevented or reduced.
3. Establish Critical Limits: Set thresholds (e.g., time, temperature) that must be met to ensure safety.
4. Establish Monitoring Procedures: Set up techniques to track CCPs.
5. Establish Corrective Actions: Determine actions to take when a deviation occurs.
6. Establish Verification Procedures: Validate that the system works effectively.
7. Establish Record-Keeping Procedures: Maintain documentation for the HACCP plan.
